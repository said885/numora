The three questions people actually ask

Almost every "percentage problem" is one of these three:

  1. Part of a whole — "what is 15% of 240?" Multiply: (15 ÷ 100) × 240 = 36.
  2. Share of a whole — "45 is what percent of 60?" Divide and scale: (45 ÷ 60) × 100 = 75%.
  3. Relative change — "it went from 80 to 100." Difference over the original: (100 − 80) ÷ 80 = +25%.

The classic trap: asymmetric change

A value that rises 50% and then falls 50% does not return to where it started — it ends 25% below the original. That is because each percentage is applied to a different base. The same effect explains why a 20% discount followed by a 20% surcharge is not a wash, and why "our rates dropped 1% from 10%" (a 10% relative drop) is a very different claim from "our rates dropped 1% from 2%".

Percentages can also exceed 100 (a company that triples revenue grew 300%) or be negative (a decrease). Both are normal — the calculator handles them without complaint.
