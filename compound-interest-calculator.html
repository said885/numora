How compounding actually works

Simple interest pays only on the original amount. Compound interest pays on the original amount plus everything already earned, every period. With monthly contributions the future value is:

FV = P × (1 + r/12)12t + C × [((1 + r/12)12t − 1) ÷ (r/12)]

where P is the initial deposit, C the monthly contribution, r the annual return as a decimal, and t the number of years. The year-by-year table above shows the two curves diverging: early on, growth is mostly your contributions; later, compounding does more work than you do.

What the numbers teach

  • Time beats amount. Starting ten years earlier often outweighs contributing twice as much — compounding has more periods to act.
  • Rate matters exponentially. The difference between 5% and 7% over 25 years is roughly 60% more final capital, not 40%.
  • Fees compound too. A 1% annual fee on the same plan can consume 20–25% of the final balance over long horizons.
  • Inflation is the reverse. At 2.5% inflation, money's purchasing power halves in ~28 years; a "safe" 0% real return is a slow loss.

Assumes a constant annual return compounded monthly, no taxes, no fees and no inflation. Real markets are volatile; this is a planning estimate, not investment advice.
